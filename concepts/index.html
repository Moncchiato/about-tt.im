The Treatment Target Identification Model

A unifying map for clinicians: instead of inventing new therapies, the TTIM helps you find what is missing in a person's life for a good lived experience, and which evidence-based targets to treat.

The paradox

Our therapies work. So why do so many people stay stuck?

Mental health interventions show consistent, moderate benefit — yet distress keeps rising and relapse is common. The TTIM proposes that our interventions don't need an overhaul. What needs refining is which targets we choose, and how we conceptualize them.

What the TTIM is

One framework, beneath every modality

The TTIM is a visual, mechanism-oriented tool for case conceptualization. It is built to be used three ways — in education, in supervision, and in individual case formulation — and it deliberately targets "poor lived experience," not just diagnosable pathology.

Targets for treatment, more than symptoms for management

The TTIM grounds intervention selection in established biological and psychological mechanisms, rather than chasing isolated symptoms or single diagnoses.

A shared language

The TTIM bridges psychological and biological conceptualizations, giving medical and mental-health providers a common map they can both read.

Easy to use, iterative, and adaptable clinical tool

Training in guiding questions can surface targets quickly, the formulation evolves as therapy unfolds, and the model expands as the evidence base does.

A note on "lived experience"

The TTIM defines lived experience as a firsthand, subjective, personal experience of/reaction to one's environment. This deliberately includes people who present for therapy not because of their own pathology, but because of distress in those around them, or because they are navigating disempowering systems and hard conditions — while still retaining assessment for psychological pathology.

Culture is a foundational aspect of the model

A framework grounded in cross-cultural science

The Theory of Constructed Emotion at the heart of the TTIM draws on research spanning diverse global populations — from Western to indigenous peoples — which shows that emotions are not universal but vary with culture, language, and context. Because of that foundation, the TTIM treats culture as a required step in every formulation rather than an afterthought. It helps clinicians distinguish what is innate human biology from what is culturally shaped, supports care when client and clinician share little common life experience, and is designed to act as a protective tool for clients who are culturally different or marginalized.

The engine underneath

Emotion is constructed — and so is suffering

The TTIM is built on the Theory of Constructed Emotion and the biology of allostasis: the brain's constant prediction of the body's needs. Emotions emerge as the brain interprets bodily affect through learned concepts. When that predictive process goes awry, distress follows.

Metabolic Health shapes the Greater Nervous System, which produces Emotion (Affect + Concept). Executive Functioning gets special attention for its power to act on concept over raw affect.

Outside the Skin

The Greater World, Culture, Relationships and Behavior bidirectionally shape — and are shaped by — what happens inside. Biology and environment are inseparable from lived experience.

Cognitions

The brain-based data — beliefs, language, schemas, education, experience — that the mind uses to make predictions during allostasis.

Beliefs / Education / Experience / Language

The learning of a lifetime shapes which predictions the brain makes. Maladaptive beliefs perpetuate distress through rigid or inaccurate interpretations of inner and outer events.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client hold any beliefs, education, experience, or language that are maladaptive to them? (Cognitive evaluation takes time; present emotional symptoms cue the exploration, e.g. the ABC model.)

Sample interventions

Cognitive restructuring · Psychoeducation · Values exploration · Selective attentional focusing · New linguistic frameworks · Corrective experiences

Cognitive Distortions

Systematic thinking errors bias information processing and keep predictive models misaligned with reality, sustaining stress reactivity and draining the body's resources.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client hold any cognitive distortions? (Refer to existing questionnaires such as the CD-Quest.)

Sample interventions

Cognitive restructuring · Behavioral experiments · Mindfulness-based metacognitive awareness

Acceptance

A mismatch between beliefs about reality and what actually happens drives dissonance, distress, and maladaptive behavior. Accepting reality is often the precondition for effective change.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client have a strong acceptance of actual reality / realistic probabilities?

Sample interventions

DBT Radical Acceptance · ACT commitment strategies · Psychoeducation · Mindfulness-based practices

Attention Regulation

Where attention goes shapes prediction. Affect-biased attention and hypervigilance maintain disordered states; trainable attentional control builds top-down regulation.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client focus on the adaptive / important aspects of their life?

Sample interventions

DBT Mindfulness skills · Focused-attention meditation · Open-monitoring · Cognitive control exercises · Behavioral activation

Locus of Control

An adaptive balance between believing one drives outcomes and recognizing external forces sustains motivation and healthy responsibility. Too far internal breeds over-responsibility and shame; too far external breeds helplessness.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client have an adaptive balance of believing they drive outcomes versus external forces driving outcomes?

Sample interventions

Problem-solving therapy · Cognitive restructuring · SAGE skill (RO-DBT) · Responsibility pie chart · Socratic reframing · Behavioral experiments · Radical Openness (RO-DBT) · Self-enquiry (RO-DBT) · Radical Acceptance · Mindfulness of the present moment

Respondent Conditioning

Learned associations drive automatic emotional and physiological reactions that operate outside conscious control — and that cognition alone often can't reach.

Guiding questions

  • Has the client's life conditioned any maladaptive responses to life situations?
  • Does the client need to engage with respondent conditioning experiences to address emotional issues?

Sample interventions

Exposure & Response Prevention · Exposure therapy · Systematic desensitization · EMDR · Interoceptive exposure · Behavioral experiments · Behavioral activation

Allostatic Load

The wear-and-tear of chronically mismanaged bodily systems — focused on the sympathetic nervous system and the dopamine system.

Control / Demand / Predictability

The primate nervous system is built to react to control, demand and predictability. Low control, low predictability or overwhelming demand drives a high allostatic load — sometimes from pathology, sometimes from oppressive systems.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client have very little control / predictability and very high demands in their life?
  • Does the client struggle with a need for control?
  • Does the client need to know what is coming, or struggle with what to expect?
  • Does the client feel resistant to external demands?

Sample interventions

Education · Exposure · Behavioral activation / modification · DBT eliciting commitment · Cognitive reframing · Mindfulness on the controllable · Problem-solving · Stress-response medication · DBT Distress Tolerance · DBT Building Mastery · Acceptance strategies · Adjusting the environment

Evaluation of Performance

The SNS mobilizes us toward performance. A poor relationship with performance — or unmet, unrealistic demands — creates sustained arousal where the effort to perform undermines performance itself.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client struggle with feelings regarding their performance in life / tasks?

Sample interventions

Cognitive restructuring of perfectionism · DBT Building Mastery · Values-based behavioral activation (ACT) · Biofeedback · Mindfulness · Exposure-based interventions

Social Hierarchy

The SNS is naturally sensitive to social rank — higher arousal when perceived as lower-status. An under-discussed driver of self-worth struggles, rejection sensitivity and chronic dysregulation.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client feel like they are a valuable or worthwhile person?
  • Does the client feel intense feelings when others question them or their worth?
  • Are there external or internal factors driving the client to feel lesser?

Sample interventions

Cognitive restructuring of status/worth beliefs · Status-based personal values · Radical Acceptance of social status · Letting-go (Buddhist or Yogic-style) approaches · Biofeedback · Mindfulness · Exposure · Supportive environments · Self-esteem building · Finding ways to be of value to others

Sympathetic Learning History / Trauma

Trauma sensitizes the nervous system to threat, producing rigid autonomic states and overgeneralized danger cues that persist long after the events. No formulation is complete without evaluating it.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client have a history of working in high-stress environments?
  • Does the client have any trauma history?

Sample interventions

Trauma-focused CBT · EMDR · Cognitive Processing Therapy · Prolonged Exposure · Corrective environments & experiences · Somatic / body-oriented therapies

Chronic SNS Activation

When stress mediators exceed coping capacity the system dysregulates into allostatic overload — surfacing as the physical complaints that so often arrive on a therapist's couch.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client wake before they intend to, or have insomnia?
  • Stomach issues? Autoimmune issues or a weak / inconsistent immune system?
  • Chronic fatigue? Memory struggles? Sexual dysfunction?
  • Muscle tension (shoulders / neck / jaw)? High blood pressure?

Sample interventions

Down-regulation / relaxation techniques · Behavioral & cognitive stress reduction · Medical collaboration · Address upstream targets (control, trauma, performance) · DBT Distress Tolerance skills · DBT Emotion Regulation skills · Mindfulness-based stress reduction · Biofeedback · Physical activity · Sleep hygiene

Dopamine System Disruption

Dopamine governs reward, motivation and movement, and operates on relative, prediction-based stimulation. Modern life over-stimulates it; under- and over-activation both produce mental-health consequences.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client feel unpleasant when prevented from doing what they want?
  • Does the client feel their best when they do new things?
  • Does the client feel their best when pursuing a goal?
  • Does the client enjoy the early stages of new things, then struggle once novelty wears off?

Sample interventions

Abstinence from highly dopaminergic activity (reset) · Aligning values to behavior · Increasing non-dopaminergic pleasures · Structural schedule adjustments · Lifestyle exploration · Pharmacology (stimulants / dopamine modulators; antagonists for excess)

Affect Tolerance

Affect varies along two axes: energy (arousal) and valence. When some regions of that space feel intolerable — high-energy distress like panic or rage, low-energy states like emptiness or boredom, even calm or joy — clients escape them through avoidance, numbing, impulsive action or dissociation. Widening the window of tolerance lets a client stay present and regulated across the full affective range.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client have the capacity to handle a range of affective experiences, including all combinations of high and low energy and high and low valence?

Sample interventions

Somatic Experiencing (titration & pendulation) · Sensorimotor Psychotherapy · Integral Somatic Psychology · Trauma-informed yoga · DBT Distress Tolerance · DBT Emotion Regulation · ACT willingness & defusion · Emotion Regulation Therapy · STAIR · AEDP / ISTDP · Emotion-Focused Therapy · IFS parts work · EMDR (incl. Positive Affect Tolerance protocol) · Accelerated Resolution Therapy

Relationships

The quality and quantity of human connection — a fundamental regulator of stress physiology and a top reason people seek therapy.

Social Homeostatic Needs

Social isolation produces real physiological consequences and rising allostatic load. Unmet social needs are statistically probable for almost anyone presenting for care.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client have close relationships (family / friend / romantic)? Do they feel lonely?
  • Is the client happy with the closeness of their relationships?
  • Is anything missing in the client's life from a social perspective?
  • How often does the client speak with and spend time with people?

Sample interventions

Collaborative problem-solving to find connection · Group therapy · Using the therapeutic relationship to scaffold social needs

Relationship Skills

Maintaining relationships is a learned, practiced skill set — communication, boundaries, conflict resolution. Deficits drive interpersonal friction and destabilize attachment.

Guiding questions

  • Do people in the client's life often find themselves upset with the client, or take advantage of the client?

Sample interventions

Social skills training · DBT Interpersonal Effectiveness · Gottman Method · Family therapy · CBT for maladaptive interpersonal patterns · Modeling in session · Conflict-resolution scripts · Mindfulness programs involving significant others

How You Make Others Feel

Technically correct social behavior can still leave others feeling unseen. Awareness of one's impact — perspective-taking and empathic accuracy — is often the missing piece.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client know what they need in relationships, and what people need from them?
  • Do people in the client's life make the client feel positive or negative?
  • Does the client find people avoiding or distancing themselves from them?

Sample interventions

Radically-Open DBT · DBT Interpersonal skills · Metacognition / mentalization training · Mindfulness of one's impact · Therapeutic-relationship feedback · Functional Analytic Psychotherapy

Experiencing Connection

The capacity to feel connection itself can be the target — for relational trauma, insecure attachment, alexithymia or autism. Therapy can be a corrective, co-regulating experience.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client feel connected to / understood by those around them?
  • Do the people around the client feel connected to / understood by them?

Sample interventions

Building a strong therapeutic alliance · Attachment-based interventions · The therapeutic relationship as a model for connection · Graded exposure to deeper connection

Purpose

Meaning, agency and a future worth moving toward — the reason a regulated, connected, healthy person can still feel that life is hollow.

Meaning for Living

A reason for living confers resilience even when immediate rewards are scarce — a force that can counteract a chronically over-active lateral habenula and pervasive hopelessness.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client have a reason for being on a day-to-day basis? On a lifelong basis?

Sample interventions

ACT values work · Narrative Therapy · Existential therapy · Meaning-centered psychotherapy

Good Things Are Coming

A global conviction that a positive future is possible. Without it, planning and effort can feel hollow — distinct from, and deeper than, looking forward to specific events.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client believe good experiences or things are coming?

Sample interventions

Cognitive restructuring of future beliefs · Behavioral activation · Psychoeducation + world engagement · Future Event Specificity Training · Mindfulness for a more optimistic outlook

Agency Over Future

Belief in influence over outcomes — an internal locus of control and an iterative mindset — sustains motivation and resilience. Its absence is learned helplessness.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client feel like they have some control over their future?

Sample interventions

CBT challenging control beliefs · Reattributing success to effort · Reframing adversity as skill-building · Psychoeducation on locus-of-control malleability · Growth mindset · Motivational interviewing · Mindful 'one step at a time' focus · DBT Building Mastery · Problem-solving & self-efficacy

Looking Forward to Experiences

Anticipating positive experiences sustains the dopamine system and the sense that life is worth living. Anhedonia blunts the drive to pursue goals at all.

Guiding questions

  • Is the client looking forward to anything?

Sample interventions

Behavioral activation (pleasurable activities) · Savoring · Mindfulness of pleasant experiences · Future Event Specificity Training · Anticipatory pleasure via guided imagery & memory recall

Purpose to Others

Contributing to others' lives builds self-worth and releases prosocial neuromodulators, lowering SNS activity and allostatic load. A modern world of frictionless services makes such roles harder to find.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client contribute to their world in any way?

Sample interventions

Volunteer work · Community engagement · DBT ACCEPTS skill · Mentorship · ACT matrix work · Acts of service

Commitment

Willingness to engage with effort and change underpins every other intervention. Enhancing a sense of control also reduces SNS activation and allostatic load. As a facet of Purpose, it is the chosen, values-driven decision to keep moving toward a life worth living.

Guiding questions

  • Does the client demonstrate the willingness to engage with effort and change?

Sample interventions

Fostering grit / iterative mindset · Behavioral activation · Motivational interviewing · DBT Willingness vs Willfulness, Willing Hands · DBT Building Mastery · Problem-solving & assertiveness

Accomplishment

The satisfaction that follows direct effort reinforces agency and reward pathways. Without earned wins, motivation and self-efficacy erode even when meaning and connection are present.

Guiding questions

  • Has the client experienced the feelings of satisfaction that follow direct effort?

Sample interventions

WOOP method · Micro-goal chunking · Progress journaling · Habit stacking · Deliberate practice · Best-Possible-Self imagery · The Progress Principle · Growth mindset

Metabolic Health

The physiological substrate of the mind — nutrition, movement, sleep, inflammation and substances. Often needs interdisciplinary collaboration.

Substance Use

Psychoactive substances alter physiological baselines, impair executive function bottom-up, and leave metabolic after-effects that can linger for months or years after cessation.

Guiding questions

  • Does your client engage in frequent substance use?

Sample interventions

Abstinence · Harm reduction · Understanding the substance's function & meeting it through lived experience · Pharmacological support

Gut Microbiome (Probiotics / Prebiotics)

Gut bacteria produce neurotransmitter precursors and short-chain fatty acids. A poor microbiome fuels inflammation and disrupts the gut-brain axis, feeding depression and anxiety.

Guiding questions

  • Does your client eat probiotic or low-sugar fermented foods?

Sample interventions

Low-sugar fermented foods · Dietary fiber · Reducing gut-harming foods · Targeted probiotic / prebiotic regimens · (Rarely) fecal transplant under medical oversight

Content of Food

The brain needs specific nutrients; deficits (omega-3, vitamin D, magnesium, B vitamins) translate into mental-health symptoms. Diet quality directly shapes mood and cognition.

Guiding questions

  • Does your client eat a lot of protein and fiber, or is their diet mostly carbohydrates?

Sample interventions

DBT PLEASE skill · Whole, unprocessed foods · Fruits, vegetables, lean protein · Targeted supplementation (omega-3, B vitamins)

Refined Sugars / Fructose Consumption

High refined-sugar and fructose intake drives neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction and insulin resistance — a modifiable risk factor across mood and cognitive disorders.

Guiding questions

  • Does your client eat a lot of foods high in refined sugars?

Sample interventions

Reducing processed foods & added sugars · DBT PLEASE skill · Metabolic-health screening · Emphasizing whole foods · Education on sugar's effects · Ketogenic metabolic therapy strategies

Inflammation

Chronic, often-undetected systemic inflammation alters neurotransmitter metabolism, impairs neuroplasticity, and is implicated in depression, bipolar disorder and treatment resistance.

Guiding questions

  • Does your client have any chronic inflammation they're aware of? (dry eyes, excess mucus, balance problems, brain fog, low energy, clotting problems, insulin resistance, skin symptoms, poor digestion, low back pain, swollen lymph nodes)

Sample interventions

Anti-inflammatory diets · Regular exercise · Cognitive / behavioral stress reduction · Targeted anti-inflammatory supplementation

Physical Activity

Perhaps the most-researched health intervention: movement modulates BDNF and neurotransmitters, builds metabolic resilience, and rivals therapy for some outcomes.

Guiding questions

  • Does your client get a lot of physical activity?

Sample interventions

Walking / running / dancing · Resistance training · Monitoring behavioral antecedents & consequences of activity

Sleep

Sleep underwrites neuroplasticity, memory and emotion regulation. Every mental-health condition shows a relationship with disturbed sleep.

Guiding questions

  • How many hours a night does the client sleep?
  • Do they sleep through the night?

Sample interventions

CBT-i · Sleep hygiene · Light exposure / chronotherapy · Adequate daytime energy expenditure · Relaxation techniques · Temperature adjustments · Treating comorbidities that disrupt sleep

Circadian Rhythm

Cortisol rhythm and sleep-wake timing can be disrupted even when sleep duration is intact — a distinct target. Modern light, screens and shift schedules are common culprits.

Guiding questions

  • Does your client get ample early-morning sunlight?
  • Does your client feel energized during the daylight hours?

Sample interventions

Light therapy · Consistent meal timing · Timed exercise · Maintaining regular social rhythms

In practice

How the TTIM works in the room

The model is meant to be used as a living cycle — surfacing targets, sharing them with the client, and planning collaboratively as the picture evolves.

1

Assess & formulate

Use the guiding questions during intake. Each flagged target invites deeper evaluation, building an idiographic picture of which pillars are in deficit.

2

Share & educate

Show the client the pillars and targets in deficit. Matter-of-fact psychoeducation reduces judgment, builds alliance, fosters hope, and is itself an intervention.

3

Plan & prioritize

Choose targets collaboratively. The most destabilizing ones (often Allostatic Load) come first. Re-prioritize as new data arrives — the cycle repeats.

Guiding logic of the TTIM: The dose determines the poison — too much, or too little

The TTIM never claims a single factor predicts all outcomes. Instead it asks where internal and external factors sit in excess or deficit, and how those imbalances ripple through a life — an idea echoed in traditional models of restoring balance and research on trauma and resilience.

A cross-cutting consideration: Executive Functioning

Executive functioning isn't a pillar — you don't need it to feel positive emotion — but it influences nearly every outcome and every session. The model keeps it visible as a consideration across all targets (e.g., CBT for ADHD, environmental structuring, bottom-up regulatory strategies). Modern research does not support the idea that interventions targetting executive functioning have long-term effects, and instead, executive functioning can be scaffolded as a part of treatment. Guiding question: Does the client experience executive functioning deficits? (e.g., use an existing tool such as the BRIEF2.)

Adaptable by design

Built to be flexing depending on practical settings — and to grow

The TTIM is not static. It is built to adapt to different practice environments and acuity levels, and to let clinicians add, move, or remove treatment targets wherever a need arises — placing each one where it belongs in the model. As new research and modalities emerge, the framework expands with them.

New biologyDrop a specific peptide or hormone target into Metabolic Health, the Greater Nervous System, or Allostatic Load for higher-acuity and psychiatric work.
Genetic influenceAdd a “Genes” category under Inside the Skin (e.g., ADHD, autism, bipolar, alexithymia) when genetic factors shape the picture. Can be helpful for education or epigenetic work.
Community practiceAdd Greater World and Culture targets for clinicians, sociologists, anthropologists, and activists working beyond the individual.
Executive-function focusExpand into sub-targets — inhibition, working memory, shifting, planning, self-monitoring — for ADHD or BPD work.

The TTIM is a theoretically grounded, evidence-based proposal — not yet an empirically validated instrument. It is intended to scaffold clinical reasoning, education, and supervision, and does not replace clinical judgment, diagnosis, or appropriate interdisciplinary collaboration (e.g., with physicians or dietitians for metabolic targets).
